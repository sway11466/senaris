DESIGN — the town's houses. This block is repeated word for word in every slot
of this skin (the board object and the combat band), so that the small clump on
the board and the big houses in the battle window are plainly the same town.

- Every house is a single-storey cottage with a steep gabled roof. The roof is
  as tall as the wall beneath it: half the height of the house is roof.
- Walls are rough grey fieldstone, #8C8A82, laid without courses, with paler
  mortar between the stones. The triangular gable carries exposed timber framing
  in weathered brown, #6E5A42 — three or four straight members, no carving, no
  curves.
- EVERY roof is grey slate and nothing else: base tone #7E8388, each house a
  slightly different shade of that same grey, some a touch lighter, some a touch
  darker. NO red, NO terracotta, NO orange, NO brown, NO ochre, NO blue, NO
  green, NO thatch, NO straw, NO reed, NO wooden shingles, NO turf. Red and blue
  roofs mark who holds a fortified place in this game, so an ordinary house may
  never wear them.
- Each roof has a plain straight ridge and eaves that overhang the wall by about
  a tenth of the house's width. No dormers, no gables added on the slope, no
  tiles picked out one by one.
- One plain plank door per house, weathered brown #6E5A42. Windows are small
  squares with shutters, at most two to a house, no glass and no lattice.
- About half the houses carry a square stone chimney at the ridge.
- NEVER in this skin: a tower, a gate, a town wall, a church, a belfry, an
  arcade, a shop front, a sign, a flag, a banner. Those belong to the town's
  fort, which has to stay tellable apart from ordinary houses.
- NEVER: people, animals, carts, barrels, crates, washing lines, smoke, fire.

SUBJECT: An ordinary STREET of that town, seen close — a WIDE BAND of backdrop
that stands behind the fighting in a battle window. It is not a single object in
the middle of the frame; it is one stretch of street, running off the left edge
of the picture.

THIS IS NOT THE TOWN'S STRONGHOLD. There is no town wall, no gate, no tower and
no hall here — those belong to the fort, and a player has to be able to tell at a
glance that this fight is happening among ordinary houses. Every roof in this
picture is a low cottage roof and they are all about the same height: NO building
stands head and shoulders above the rest.

WHAT IS IN IT — a row of four or five squat cottages packed shoulder to shoulder
along the far side of the street, their front walls facing the viewer. Widths and
depths vary and the fronts step slightly in and out, so the row is never a flat
even wall, but the ridges stay within a hand's breadth of each other.

THE LANE — a narrow lane runs back between the houses, its mouth about a third of
the way in from the RIGHT end of the picture. Inside the lane the walls close in
and the buildings get smaller and darker as they recede, so the town plainly
continues past this row. This gap is what makes the picture read as a street
rather than a solid wall of building. The floor of the lane may be seen inside
the gap; nowhere else is any ground drawn.

THE ROOFS are the most important shapes: four or five of them large and clearly
readable, overlapping each other as the houses step back. Nothing pokes above the
top of the band; leave a little clear space over the highest ridge.

THE TWO ENDS ARE DIFFERENT — this is the most important thing.
- LEFT end: the row of houses runs straight off the edge of the picture and is
  cut by it. Nothing tapers, nothing ends, nothing is rounded off there.
- RIGHT end: the street has to finish inside the picture. Finish it honestly: the
  last cottage turns its corner and the row runs away from the viewer, the
  buildings behind it getting smaller and lower as they recede, and the last of
  them fading out. Do NOT stop the row in mid-air with a raw broken end.

SHAPE AND PLACE IN THE FRAME — the picture is square. The band of street fills
the BOTTOM HALF of it, edge to edge: twice as wide as it is tall. In a
1024-pixel picture the street is about 512 pixels tall and 1024 pixels wide. It
touches the left edge, the right edge and the bottom edge. Everything above it is
background.

THE BOTTOM EDGE — the front walls of the houses are cut flat by the bottom edge
of the picture. Draw nothing below them: no street surface, no cobbles, no
paving, no ground, no grass, no soil, no cast shadow. The walls must run all the
way down to the very bottom edge and be cut by it — no strip of background along
the bottom. Soldiers will stand in front of the houses and their heads will
overlap the bottom of the walls.

VIEWING ANGLE — seen from a slight high three-quarter angle, as if looking down
onto the street from above and in front, but much closer than a map: the front
walls, the doors and the mouth of the lane are clearly readable, and the roofs
are still fully in view above them.

THE BACKGROUND — everything above the roofline is PURE FLAT WHITE, #FFFFFF, with
NO texture, NO shading, NO gradient, NO sky, NO clouds. This white is going to be
deleted, so the roofline must be a hard, clean, crisp edge against it. Nothing in
the street may be white or near-white.

NO SHADOWS AND NO LIGHT DIRECTION — read this twice, it was broken last time.
There is NO light source anywhere in this picture.

- A wall is ONE flat tone across its whole face: no gradient, no darker corner,
  no dirt or shading at the base, no glow along the top.
- A roof has exactly TWO tones — the two slopes either side of the ridge, one a
  little lighter than the other — and the lighter slope is on the SAME side on
  every roof in the picture.
- NOTHING casts a shadow onto anything: no shadow under the eaves, no shadow in
  the angle where two houses meet, no shadow of a near house falling on a far
  wall, no shadow on the ground (there is no ground). Where a near cottage
  overlaps a far one, the far wall is simply hidden behind it — draw no dark
  band at the join.
- The gable timbers, the door and the shutters are flat brown shapes, not lit
  ones. The stones of a wall may differ slightly in tone from one another, but
  no group of them may darken together into a shaded patch.
- This picture is mirrored left to right when the game lays it down, so a baked
  light direction would end up pointing both ways on the same board.

This is a near view, so a little more detail than the map piece is welcome — the
courses of the stonework, the boards of a door, the ridge line of a roof — but
keep it in bold flat shapes, with no fine texture that would fight with the
figures standing in front of it.
