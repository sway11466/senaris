STYLE: A side-on texture for a fantasy tactics game, in the same clean stylized
cel-shaded look as the game's terrain and unit art: bold flat shading, a mature,
slightly muted, limited color palette (NOT bright saturated, NOT painterly
photorealism). Viewed straight from the FRONT, face-on — a flat lay with NO
perspective, NO horizon, NO sky, NO ground line, NO floor, NO grass. The texture
fills the ENTIRE square frame edge to edge, with NO border, NO frame, NO
vignette, and no single big focal object. It must tile SEAMLESSLY LEFT TO RIGHT:
the left and right edges continue into each other. CRITICAL: vary the pieces
RANDOMLY and UNEVENLY — never a repeated motif, never the same shape twice,
never in rows or a regular grid. Avoid large, high-contrast patches (they repeat
visibly when tiled); keep large-scale color even and low-contrast. Square 1:1.

SUBJECT: The cut FLANK of a heap of graveyard rubble, as if the pile had been
sliced open and you are looking straight at the exposed side. This is an
ASSEMBLY OF LOOSE SEPARATE PIECES piled on each other — never one solid body of
rock. What faces you is mostly the THICKNESS of flat stone: the edges of broken
headstone slabs, crypt roof slabs and floor slabs lying at every angle, some
nearly flat, some tipped steeply, some standing almost on end, crossing and
propping each other. Between and behind them are squared blocks seen end-on,
snapped chunks, and the small chips and grit that have trickled down into the
voids. Everything is wedged and unstable-looking, resting on whatever is beneath
it. This is the DEEP BOTTOM of the heap over the whole frame — rubble that has
settled for years and compacted under its own weight. Everywhere, edge to edge,
the pieces have slumped into each other until they touch: slabs lie flush along
their neighbours, chunks have dropped into every hollow, and chips and grit have
trickled down and filled what was left. Do NOT make the top of the frame more
open than the bottom, and do NOT run any change of density from top to bottom or
from side to side. The density is the same everywhere, just with the clumping
varied. Almost NO dark shows through: what gaps remain are hairline seams and
narrow slots between touching pieces — NO open pockets, NO black holes, NO
caverns, and never a patch of dark bigger than one of the small chips.

A couple of old bones or a pale skull may be caught among the stones — this came
out of a burial ground — but keep them FEW, SMALL and as pale as the stone. NO
carving, NO inscriptions, NO writing, NO names, NO symbols on any piece.

STRICTLY FORBIDDEN: NOT a built wall — NO regular courses, NO stacked brick
pattern, NO mortar lines, NO fitted joints, NO worked or dressed faces, NO
foundation, NO capping row. NOT one continuous rock face — every piece must be
plainly separate, with its own outline, from the ones around it. Also NO
classical or temple architecture of any kind: NO columns, NO column drums, NO
fluted stone, NO capitals, NO cornices, NO moulded edges, NO pediments. NO
natural boulders, NO cobbles, NO gravel bed, NO cliff face. NO earth, NO soil,
NO mud, NO roots, NO moss, NO lichen, NO plants of any kind. NO coffins, NO
props, NO tools, NO metal.

The stone is a dull weathered grey-tan, hex #A79E90 — the same stone as the
graveyard's markers, a shade deeper than the sunlit top of the heap because this
is its shaded inner side. Each piece takes a slightly different flat tone
somewhere between #B4AC9E and #968D80, so the pieces separate from one another
by tone alone.

CRITICAL — the light comes from DIRECTLY ABOVE and from nowhere else. This strip
is wrapped around all six sides of a hexagon, so any sideways light would
contradict itself: NO light from the left, NO light from the right, NO highlight
or gradient running sideways across the frame, NO cast shadows thrown to one
side. Upward-facing edges may catch a little light and the undersides of pieces
may sit a little darker — that is the only shading allowed, and it must be the
same everywhere across the width.

Piece size varies but stays SMALL — big slabs leave big gaps, so keep them out:
the largest slab edges are about one tenth of the frame height, most are well
under that, down to fist-sized chips and grit. Arrange them irregularly, NEVER
evenly spaced, NEVER in rows, NEVER the same shape twice.

CRITICAL, and the easiest thing to get wrong: the picture must stay LIGHT
OVERALL — squint at it and it should read as pale stone, never a dark grey or
brown picture. Dark voids and dark outlines are what drag the average down, so
keep every dark accent both FEW and THIN. If in doubt, use fewer of them and
make them paler.
